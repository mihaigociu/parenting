# Research Notes — Phase 1: Meta-Analyses & Systematic Reviews

This file captures the highest-tier evidence syntheses on journaling-adjacent interventions for school-age children: expressive writing, gratitude interventions/diaries, and writing instruction in primary grades.

**Headline takeaway from Phase 1:** The evidence base for "journaling helps primary school kids" is real but modest. Effect sizes are small (Hedges' g ≈ 0.13–0.27 across the most relevant meta-analyses). The strongest signals come from gratitude diaries and dialogue-style classroom writing; the weakest from generic free expressive writing imported uncritically from adult Pennebaker-paradigm research. Primary-school-only evidence (ages 5–11) is thin — most "youth" meta-analyses lean adolescent.

---

## 1. Travagin, Margola & Revenson (2015) — Expressive writing in adolescents

**Citation:** Travagin, G., Margola, D., & Revenson, T. A. (2015). How effective are expressive writing interventions for adolescents? A meta-analytic review. *Clinical Psychology Review*, 36, 42–55.
**URL:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0272735815000161 (abstract; full text paywalled)
**Also referenced via:** https://www.researchgate.net/publication/271225550 and https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/548e92b8b7d2a0e40d77789fd819b9911bff4f77

### Scope
- 21 studies meeting inclusion criteria (26 identified)
- Ages 10–18 — adolescents, not primary school. **This is the single most-cited youth meta-analysis on expressive writing, but it excludes children younger than 10.**
- Outcome domains: emotional distress, problem behavior, social adjustment, school participation

### Effect sizes
- **Overall mean Hedges' g = 0.127** (small but positive)
- Domain-specific g = 0.107 to 0.246
- Authors describe the evidence as "promising but not decisive"

### Implications for ages 5–11
- The youngest participants are 10. Effects below age 10 cannot be inferred from this review.
- Authors note that younger participants tended to benefit *less* from disclosing negative experiences — relevant caveat for early-primary children.
- Larger effects observed in subgroups with higher pre-intervention stress, poorer physical health, or lower optimism.

### Methodological caveats
- Heterogeneity across studies in writing protocol, dose, and outcome measures.
- The Pennebaker expressive-writing paradigm (write deeply about a stressful event for 15–20 min on 3–4 occasions) was developed for adults and adapted downward — its developmental appropriateness for children is contested.

## 2. Amos, Moase & Sladeczek (2025) — School-based expressive writing scoping review

**Citation:** Amos, J., Moase, J., & Sladeczek, I. E. (2025). A scoping review of school-based expressive writing implementation reporting practices: missed opportunities and new research directions. *Discover Mental Health*.
**URL:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11880461/

### Scope
- 19 eligible studies of expressive writing in school settings
- Spans primary, elementary, middle, and high school
- **Only 3 studies were on primary/elementary specifically** — confirms how thin the under-10 evidence is

### Findings
- Positive effects on "internalizing behaviour, problem behaviour, and school participation"
- Mean Hedges' g effect sizes: **0.107 to 0.246**
- "Effectiveness varies considerably across studies with some individual studies obtaining negative results"
- 95% of studies adapted (modified) the original Pennebaker EW paradigm — making it hard to isolate which components drive effects

### Implementation insight (most useful for parents)
- Only one of 19 studies reported all five minimum implementation domains (recruitment, reach, dose delivered, dose received, fidelity)
- **"Effect sizes are two to three times larger when serious implementation issues are avoided"** — i.e. *how* journaling is introduced matters as much as *whether* it is done
- Six studies found "contamination": participants did not follow writing instructions as intended (e.g. wrote superficial content) — implies passive prescription doesn't work

### Quote-worthy
The scoping review documents that *how* expressive writing is implemented in schools — including buy-in, dose, and fidelity — predicts whether it has a measurable effect at all.

## 3. Renshaw & Olinger Steeves (2016) — Gratitude in youth and schools

**Citation:** Renshaw, T. L., & Olinger Steeves, R. M. (2016). What good is gratitude in youth and schools? A systematic review and meta-analysis of correlates and intervention outcomes. *Psychology in the Schools*, 53(3), 286–305.
**URL:** https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/pits.21903 (paywalled abstract); ERIC: https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1090273

### Scope
- 20 empirical journal articles on gratitude in youth, through Sept 2014
- Two strands: (a) correlates of gratitude, (b) intervention outcomes

### Findings — correlates
- **Medium-sized positive correlations** between gratitude and well-being indicators in youth:
  - positive affect r = 0.40
  - life satisfaction r = 0.45
  - happiness r = 0.37
- Smaller negative correlations with depression (r = −0.28) and negative affect (r = −0.17)
- Caveat: gratitude *measures* have "relatively poor test-retest reliability and/or predictive validity"

### Findings — interventions
- **The overall effect of gratitude interventions on well-being outcomes was NOT statistically significant** in this 2016 meta-analysis
- Authors attribute the null finding to heterogeneity of protocols and dependent variables across studies
- More recent reviews (see Choi 2025 below) find a small positive effect

### Implication
- Gratitude as a *trait* correlates with well-being in youth (medium effect)
- Gratitude *interventions* in 2016 review showed unreliable effects — sensitive to how the intervention is structured
- Newer evidence (below) is more favourable but still small in magnitude

## 4. Diniz et al. (2023) — General gratitude interventions meta-analysis

**Citation:** Diniz, G., Korkes, L., Tristão, L. S., et al. (2023). The effects of gratitude interventions: a systematic review and meta-analysis. *Einstein (São Paulo)*, 21, eRW0371.
**URL:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10393216/

### Scope
- 64 studies, samples 23–1,337
- Heterogeneous populations (children with depression, teenagers, adults, prisoners, healthcare workers, chronic-illness patients)
- Interventions: gratitude diaries, conversation programs, gratitude visits, written and verbal expressions of gratitude

### Effect sizes (mean differences, not standardized)
| Outcome | Effect | 95% CI |
|---|---|---|
| Gratitude scores | +3.67% | 0.74 to 2.35 |
| Life satisfaction | +6.86% | 0.21 to 0.75 |
| Mental health | +5.8% | 0.17 to 0.41 |
| Anxiety symptoms | −7.76% | −2.37 to −0.89 |
| Depression symptoms | −6.89% | −2.89 to −0.83 |

### Notable findings on practice
- No single form of gratitude practice clearly dominated — diaries, written expressions, verbal expressions, and "counting blessings" interventions all showed positive results
- Does *not* isolate child/youth-only effect sizes

### Implication
- Across populations, gratitude practices including diaries have a consistent small-to-modest positive signal on anxiety, depression, and life satisfaction
- The specific format matters less than the consistency of practice

## 5. Choi, Cha, McCullough, Coles & Oishi (2025) — PNAS cross-cultural gratitude meta-analysis

**Citation:** Choi, H., Cha, Y., McCullough, M. E., Coles, N. A., & Oishi, S. (2025). A meta-analysis of the effectiveness of gratitude interventions on well-being across cultures. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*, 122(28), e2425193122.
**URL:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12280877/ • https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2425193122

### Scope
- **145 papers, 163 samples, 24,804 participants across 28 countries** — the largest synthesis to date
- 727 effect sizes analyzed
- Average sample age 29.5 (SD 13.4) — heavily adult-leaning

### Effect sizes
- **Overall Hedges' g = 0.19** (95% CI [0.15, 0.22]), p < 0.001 — small but robust
- Strongest effects: positive affect (g = 0.27), composite well-being (g = 0.28)

### Age findings
- **Age did NOT significantly moderate effects** — gratitude interventions appear roughly equally effective across age groups in the studies analyzed
- However: the synthesis does not isolate primary-school-aged children as a category
- Caveat: most adult studies; child-only sub-analysis would require Renshaw 2016 or newer school-specific work

### Limitations
- English-language studies only
- Six well-being outcomes; excludes anxiety
- Cross-cultural framing uses country as proxy

### Implication
- The most current and largest meta-analysis finds gratitude interventions reliably produce small positive effects on well-being
- Generalization to 5–11 age range is plausible but not directly tested

## 6. Graham, McKeown, Kiuhara & Harris (2012) — K-5 writing instruction meta-analysis

**Citation:** Graham, S., McKeown, D., Kiuhara, S., & Harris, K. R. (2012). A meta-analysis of writing instruction for students in the elementary grades. *Journal of Educational Psychology*, 104(4), 879–896.
**URL:** https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ994038 • https://psycnet.apa.org/record/2012-18075-001

### Scope
- 115 documents with computable effect sizes
- 13 different writing intervention types analyzed (each tested in ≥4 studies)
- Elementary grades only

### Effect sizes (selected)
| Intervention | Effect Size |
|---|---|
| Self-Regulated Strategy Development (SRSD) | 1.17 |
| Strategy instruction | 1.02 |
| Creativity/imagery instruction | 0.70 |
| Text structure instruction | 0.59 |
| Adding self-regulation to strategy instruction | 0.50 |
| Teaching transcription skills | 0.55 |
| **Extra writing (just write more)** | **0.30** |
| Grammar instruction alone | not significant |

### Critical finding for journaling
- **Journaling is NOT in the analyzed categories.** This is a striking absence — when one of the foundational K-5 writing-research syntheses examined 13 intervention types, free or personal journaling did not have enough studies to be analyzed as a category.
- The closest proxy is "extra writing" (ES = 0.30) — meaningful but well below targeted instruction (ES = 1.0+)

### Implication
- For writing-skill development per se, structured instruction outperforms more journaling by a wide margin
- Journaling's value for primary schoolers is likely *not* primarily as a literacy intervention — it should be evaluated on emotional/reflective grounds, not as a substitute for writing instruction

## 7. Kim, Yang, Reyes & Connor (2021) — K-3 writing instruction meta-analysis

**Citation:** Kim, Y.-S. G., Yang, D., Reyes, M., & Connor, C. (2021). Writing instruction improves students' writing skills differentially depending on focal instruction and children: A meta-analysis for primary grade students. *Educational Research Review*, 34, 100408.
**URL:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9390887/

### Scope
- 24 studies, 166 effect sizes, N = 5,589
- K–Grade 3 only — very directly relevant to early primary

### Findings
- **Overall effect of writing instruction**: 0.31 (moderate, positive)
- SRSD: 1.04 (writing quality), 0.59 (productivity), 0.72 (other)
- Transcription instruction alone: not significant
- Mixed instruction: 0.23–0.58

### Journaling coverage
- Again, **journaling not analyzed as a distinct intervention type** in this 2021 review — consistent gap

### Implication
- Reinforces Graham 2012: structured strategy instruction has the largest writing-skill effects in early primary
- The literature does not treat journaling as a literacy intervention

## 8. Two primary-school-specific gratitude diary studies (smaller-scale, but directly on the population)

### 8a. Diebel (2014) — University of Southampton

**Citation:** Diebel, T. (2014). The effectiveness of a gratitude diary intervention on primary school children's sense of school belonging. University of Southampton thesis.
**URL:** https://blog.soton.ac.uk/edpsych/2017/04/09/the-effectiveness-of-a-gratitude-diary-intervention-on-primary-school-childrens-sense-of-school-belonging/

- Children mean age 9.4 (SD 0.47), three primary schools, 2-week intervention
- Diary: either "things grateful for in school that day" (intervention) or "neutral school events" (control)
- Finding: "trend towards an increased feeling of belonging" — directional, not strongly significant in main analysis
- Note: nostalgia proneness moderated the effect

### 8b. Cripps — BPS Educational Psychology

**Citation:** Cripps, D. L. Exploring the effectiveness of a school-based gratitude intervention on children's levels of anxiety, sense of school belonging and sleep quality. British Psychological Society Educational Psychology Abstracts.
**URL:** https://www.bps.org.uk/exploring-effectiveness-school-based-gratitude-intervention-childrens-levels-anxiety-sense-school

- Year 6 (~age 11), n = 164, six primary schools in South-East England
- Daily diary: three things they were thankful for vs neutral events control
- Significant group differences favouring intervention: **anxiety, gratitude proneness, school belonging**
- **No significant difference on sleep quality** (smaller n=58 parent reports)
- Caveat: authors note "limited evidence to suggest that PPIs [positive psychology interventions] increase resilience in young people" generally — this study a positive single result, not a confirmed pattern

## Cross-cutting observations after Phase 1

1. **The literature on journaling for ages 5–11 specifically is shallow.** Most meta-analyses lean adolescent or adult, with primary-school participants representing a minority of studies.

2. **Effect sizes are uniformly small.** Across the strongest meta-analyses, Hedges' g ≈ 0.13–0.27. Real but modest. Anyone claiming journaling will transform a child is overselling.

3. **The largest writing-skill meta-analyses don't analyze journaling.** Graham (2012) and Kim (2021) — the K–5 and K–3 syntheses — did not have enough journaling studies to treat it as a category. Journaling's value is *not* well-evidenced as a literacy intervention.

4. **Implementation matters more than format.** Amos 2025 finds effect sizes 2–3x larger when implementation is high-fidelity. Diniz 2023 finds no clear winner among diary/written/verbal gratitude formats. The "how" matters more than the "which."

5. **Gratitude diaries have the most directly-applicable evidence for primary-aged children.** Two UK primary-school studies (Diebel 2014; Cripps) show positive signals on anxiety and school belonging — but small n, single-site, short follow-up.

6. **Gratitude versus expressive writing distinction is important.** "Write three things you're grateful for" (gratitude) and "write deeply about a stressful event" (expressive/Pennebaker) are different interventions. The first has more positive child-evidence; the second is mostly an adult paradigm with mixed pediatric translation.

7. **Open questions to drive Phases 2–5:**
   - Are there any long-term (>6 month) studies of journaling effects in primary kids?
   - What does the literacy-by-volume-of-writing literature actually show for this age?
   - When does expressive writing risk rumination in children?
   - What does school SEL evidence (CASEL, EEF) say about reflective writing components?
