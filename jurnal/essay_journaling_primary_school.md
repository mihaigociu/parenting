# Does Journaling Help Primary-School Children? An Evidence Review

*For parents weighing whether to introduce a journaling practice to children ages 5–11.*

---

## Executive summary

**The verdict, in one sentence:** There is a real but modest evidence base for journaling as a beneficial practice for primary-school children — strong enough to recommend, weak enough that anyone claiming dramatic effects is overselling.

**Confidence:** Moderate, with important caveats below.

**The two most important findings to know before starting:**

1. **Format matters enormously.** Gratitude-style writing ("three things you're grateful for," "what went well today") shows small positive effects on anxiety, mood, and well-being in school-age children. By contrast, Pennebaker-style expressive writing about stressful events — the foundation of adult journaling research — has been shown to *increase* anxiety in primary-aged children in at least one well-designed study. The default for a child should not be "write your feelings"; it should be "write what you're grateful for or noticed today."

2. **Privacy is not a courtesy — it is a treatment ingredient.** The single largest journaling meta-analysis found that effect sizes are *bigger* when journals are not collected or read. A child's journal that a parent reads is not the same intervention as one a parent doesn't.

**The recommendation:** Yes, introduce a journal — with the right format, the right privacy, and modest expectations. The expected benefit is real but small (Cohen's d typically 0.20–0.45 in school-age studies). It is a low-cost, low-risk practice with measurable upside if implemented well, and the implementation details determine whether it works or backfires.

---

## What the evidence supports

### Social-emotional benefits — the strongest case

Across multiple studies, gratitude-focused journaling in school-age children produces:

- **Reduced anxiety and social anxiety** — Hu et al. (2025), an RCT with 165 schoolchildren ages 10–15, found 10 minutes of gratitude-focused writing weekly for 7 weeks reduced social anxiety vs control with Cohen's d = −0.45.[^1]
- **Reduced depression symptoms** — same study, Cohen's d = −0.45.[^1]
- **Increased life satisfaction, optimism, school satisfaction** — Froh, Sefick & Emmons (2008), 221 early adolescents, "counting blessings" intervention.[^2]
- **Improved sense of school belonging** — UK primary school studies (Diebel 2014; Cripps via BPS), Year 6 children writing daily gratitude diaries showed significant gains in belonging, gratitude proneness, and anxiety.[^3][^4]
- **General well-being** — Choi et al. (2025), the largest meta-analysis of gratitude interventions to date (24,804 participants across 28 countries), found overall Hedges' g = 0.19 — small but robust. Age did not moderate effects.[^5]

The general gratitude-intervention meta-analysis by Diniz et al. (2023) — covering 64 studies including children — reported a 7.76% reduction in anxiety symptoms and 6.89% reduction in depression symptoms across studies.[^6]

These are not dramatic effects. They are small and reliable. Across the literature, the pattern holds: gratitude journaling and gratitude-adjacent practices in school-age children produce small, consistent, positive effects on emotional well-being.

### Cognitive and academic benefits — a weaker, more nuanced case

The most striking finding here is what *isn't* in the literature. Two of the most authoritative meta-analyses of writing instruction in primary grades — Graham, McKeown, Kiuhara & Harris (2012, K-5)[^7] and Kim, Yang, Reyes & Connor (2021, K-3)[^8] — analyzed dozens of writing interventions but **did not have enough studies on journaling to include it as a category**. The closest proxy ("extra writing") had an effect size of just 0.30 — meaningful but far below targeted strategy instruction (1.0+).

This means: **if your goal is to improve your child's writing skill, journaling is not the highest-evidence intervention.** Structured writing strategy programs and explicit handwriting/spelling instruction have 3–5x larger effects.

That said, journaling has plausible cognitive-adjacent value through several mechanisms:

- **Handwriting itself.** James & Engelhardt (2012) found that printing letters by hand — but not typing or tracing — uniquely activated reading-network brain regions in pre-literate 4–5-year-olds.[^9] A handwritten journal at primary school age carries this neural-developmental signal; a typed one does not.
- **Writing volume and fluency.** Reading researcher Timothy Shanahan explicitly endorses "non-stop writing" intervals (1–2 minutes daily) as a fluency-building practice, drawing on Graham et al. (2012) and recent work by Skar et al. (2024).[^10] Free-write journaling is functionally similar.
- **Dialogue journals** (sustained written exchange between teacher and student) show practitioner-reported gains in fluency, vocabulary, and motivation — particularly for EAL learners. Pham & Tran (2021) showed reflective journal writing significantly improved writing fluency and reduced errors in Vietnamese 8–10-year-olds.[^11][^12]
- **Writing as applied metacognition.** Reflective journaling engages exactly the executive-function and metacognition skills that develop most rapidly through primary years (working memory, inhibition, self-monitoring). This connection is theoretically supported by the SRL/EF literature but not directly tested by isolating journaling as a causal ingredient.

### Bottom line on benefits

Modest. Real. The strongest case is for emotional well-being via gratitude content; the weakest is for literacy via journaling alone. The handwriting evidence is strong enough to make handwritten journals preferable to typed ones, especially in early primary.

## What works in practice

### The protocol that aligns with the evidence

- **5–10 minutes per session**, 2–3 times per week. Daily is fine but not required.[^13]
- **Sustained for at least 30 days** before expecting visible benefit. Depression effects in Sohal et al. (2022) required >30 days; gratitude RCTs typically run 6–8 weeks.[^13]
- **Gratitude or "what went well / what I noticed" prompts** as default, not open-ended emotional content.
- **Handwritten** rather than typed, especially under age 9.[^9]
- **For pre-writers** (ages 5–7): drawing journals, draw-and-label notebooks. Direct RCT evidence for this age group is thin, but the underlying handwriting/letter-formation evidence supports the practice.
- **Validate, do not correct.** The dialogue-journal literature is unanimous: corrective marking transforms the journal into another school assignment and erodes the practice.[^11]

### Privacy as treatment ingredient

The Sohal et al. (2022) meta-analysis found that **effect sizes were larger when journals were not collected or analyzed**.[^13] This is congruent with developmental research showing that parental privacy invasion predicts reduced child disclosure — not increased — and erodes the parent-child trust that underpins healthy emotional development.[^14]

**Practical rule:** Establish the privacy boundary explicitly at the start. "This is yours. I will not read it unless I am genuinely worried about your safety." Honor it even when curious.

### Adult involvement — the apparent contradiction

The literature pulls in two directions:
- For *literacy and motivation*: adult engagement helps (the dialogue-journal mechanism — teacher or parent writing back to the child).
- For *emotional well-being*: adult monitoring shrinks effect sizes.

These are measuring different outcomes and can coexist. A child can have **both** a private journal that parents do not read **and** a separate shared dialogue journal with a parent or teacher — they serve different purposes and should not be merged.

### Voluntary, not mandatory

The Amos et al. (2025) scoping review found "contamination" — children not following expressive-writing instructions — in 6 of 19 studies.[^15] Mandatory journaling that the child resists tends to produce performative writing with minimal benefit. Offer, model, scaffold; do not force.

## Where the evidence is thin or contested

### Risk 1: Expressive writing about stressful content can backfire

This is the most policy-relevant finding in the entire research. **Mesghina & Richland (2020)**, a study of 250 children ages 10–12, found that expressive writing about a stressful event *increased* anxiety relative to control, and that this increased anxiety mediated *reduced* learning in a subsequent math lesson.[^16] The most-harmed subgroup was higher-achieving, higher-working-memory girls.

The authors' interpretation: emotion-regulation skills are still developing in primary-aged children. Writing about negative content without scaffolding can keep the negative state active (rumination) rather than process it.

This finding mirrors an adult study by Sbarra et al. (2013), which showed that expressive writing actually *impeded* emotional recovery in recently separated adults who scored high on rumination.[^17] The pediatric and adult ruminator data jointly suggest: **for individuals whose emotion regulation isn't doing the integrative work, writing about stressful content can intensify rather than resolve distress.**

A counter-example: Fartoukh & Chanquoy (2020) found that 4 sustained expressive-writing sessions over 2 weeks improved working memory and reduced anxiety symptoms in 5th-graders.[^18] The reconciliation is that *sustained, supportive school-context* expressive writing differs from *one-shot acute* expressive writing — context appears to moderate the effect.

**The practical takeaway:** for a child without strong emotion-regulation skills (i.e., most primary-aged children), default to positive content. Negative content is not categorically off-limits, but it needs an adult scaffold — dialogue, not solo writing.

### Risk 2: Equity and access

Dysgraphia affects roughly 5–20% of learners and frequently co-occurs with ADHD and dyslexia.[^19] For these children, a mandatory handwriting journal can become an anxiety source. Accommodations the literature supports: speech-to-text, audio journaling, drawing-with-minimal-text, short sessions. The "no correction" rule still applies.

For EAL learners, the dialogue-journal literature is actually more developed than for native speakers — and shows consistent benefit for language acquisition and reduced writing anxiety.[^11]

### Risk 3: Children with diagnosed conditions

For children with diagnosed anxiety, depression, or trauma, unsupervised journaling — especially expressive-writing-style about negative content — is not a self-help substitute for professional support. Sohal et al. (2022) found small effects of journaling for clinical conditions (5% symptom reduction) but explicitly recommended a professional context.[^13]

### Limits of what we know

- **Primary-school-only evidence is genuinely thin.** Most "youth" studies are 10+. Direct RCT evidence on journaling for ages 5–7 barely exists.
- **Long-term outcomes are not well studied.** Most studies follow children for weeks; we don't have good data on whether a journaling habit established at age 8 affects outcomes at 18.
- **Effect-size precision is limited** by heterogeneous protocols. Most meta-analyses report I² (heterogeneity) above 80%.
- **The literature does not systematically measure harms.** Sohal 2022 reports "no adverse effects" but adverse-event measurement in this field is poor. Mesghina 2020 and Sbarra 2013 demonstrate harm exists in specific contexts.
- **The mechanism of effect is not clearly established.** Is it the writing? The reflection? The relationship (in dialogue journals)? The slowing-down? Likely all of these, but the relative contributions are unknown.

## Practical recommendation for a parent

If you are considering introducing a journal to a primary-school-aged child:

1. **Yes, do it.** The evidence supports a small, reliable benefit. It is low-cost and low-risk if implemented well.

2. **Frame it as gratitude or noticing, not feelings.** Default prompts: "Three things I'm grateful for today." "One thing that went well." "Something I noticed." Save open-ended emotional prompts for when the child specifically asks to use them.

3. **Use a paper notebook, handwritten.** Especially under age 9. The handwriting itself matters.

4. **5–10 minutes, 2–3 times a week.** Build the habit slowly. Daily can come later if the child wants it.

5. **Make it explicitly private.** Tell the child you won't read it. Then don't.

6. **Never correct.** Not spelling, not grammar, not content. The journal is not an assignment.

7. **Model the practice yourself.** A parent visibly journaling is more powerful than an instructed child.

8. **Offer; never force.** If the child resists, don't compel. The practice depends on buy-in.

9. **Adapt for difficulty.** For dysgraphia / ADHD / writing struggles: try speech-to-text, audio, or drawing. For EAL: a dialogue journal with you in their second language is supported by the strongest available evidence.

10. **If your child is going through something difficult**, don't rely on the journal to do emotional processing alone. Pair it with conversation — written or oral. For more serious situations, involve a professional.

11. **Expect small effects.** Not transformation. Reliable, modest, gradual benefit to mood, well-being, and writing fluency.

12. **Consider a separate "dialogue journal" with you** — a notebook you both write in, asking and answering questions. This is a different practice from a private journal and serves different goals (connection, language modeling, motivation to write). Keep the two separate.

---

## What this research did *not* find

- No evidence that journaling at age 5–11 produces measurable long-term cognitive, academic, or psychological gains beyond the short follow-up windows of available studies.
- No evidence that journaling outperforms targeted writing instruction for literacy outcomes.
- No evidence that any specific journaling product, app, or curriculum is reliably superior to a plain notebook.
- No evidence that "venting" emotional content in writing reliably reduces distress in children — and some evidence to the contrary.

These absences are not arguments against journaling. They are the boundaries of confident claims.
