# Research Notes — Phase 2: Cognitive & Academic Evidence

This file covers what we know about journaling's effects on the cognitive/academic side: writing skill, literacy, executive function/metacognition, and the handwriting-versus-typing question.

**Headline takeaway from Phase 2:** Journaling is **not** a strong literacy intervention compared to targeted strategy instruction. But it has plausible *adjacent* cognitive value through three mechanisms: (a) more writing-time on task (small but real effect), (b) handwriting itself recruits brain networks relevant to literacy and memory, and (c) writing functions as "applied metacognition" — useful for self-regulation development. The dialogue-journal literature (teacher-student written conversation) has the most direct evidence of literacy and motivation gains, but most rigorous studies are old or in ESL contexts.

---

## 1. Writing as a literacy intervention — what the rigorous evidence says

### 1a. Shanahan on writing fluency (Reading Rockets)
**Citation:** Shanahan, T. (2022, January 8; re-issued May 3, 2025). How to teach writing fluency. *Reading Rockets — Shanahan on Literacy*.
**URL:** https://www.readingrockets.org/blogs/shanahan-on-literacy/how-teach-writing-fluency

Key claims (drawing on Graham et al. 2012 and Skar et al. 2024):
- **Transcription skills (handwriting, spelling) are the primary fluency drivers in primary grades** — more so than amount of writing
- "Issues like handwriting and spelling are more important than amount of writing when it comes to fluency" (paraphrasing Skar et al. 2024)
- **Non-stop writing intervals (1–2 minutes, no stopping or revising) are explicitly recommended** as a fluency-building practice — this is journaling-adjacent
- Volume should be distributed across the curriculum, not concentrated in writing class
- Multiple brief daily writing sessions > one long session

**Implication for journaling:** Daily short journal entries (1–5 min) likely contribute modestly to fluency by providing low-stakes practice — but they are *not* a substitute for explicit handwriting and spelling instruction in early primary. The "non-stop writing" technique Shanahan endorses is essentially the same mechanic as free-write journaling.

### 1b. Recap of meta-analytic gap (from Phase 1)
- Graham, McKeown, Kiuhara & Harris (2012) K-5 meta-analysis: 13 intervention types analyzed, **journaling not among them** — insufficient studies
- Kim, Yang, Reyes & Connor (2021) K-3 meta-analysis: same gap
- "Extra writing" as a generic intervention: ES = 0.30 (Graham 2012) — meaningful but well below targeted strategy instruction (ES = 1.0+)

**Conclusion:** If a parent's primary motivation is "improve my kid's writing skills," the evidence supports structured writing strategy programs (SRSD-style), explicit handwriting/spelling instruction, and *some* daily writing volume. Journaling alone is not the highest-evidence intervention for writing skill — but it's not zero, either.

## 2. Handwriting itself — the underlying-mechanism evidence

### 2a. James & Engelhardt (2012) — pre-literate children
**Citation:** James, K. H., & Engelhardt, L. (2012). The effects of handwriting experience on functional brain development in pre-literate children. *Trends in Neuroscience and Education*, 1(1), 32–42.
**URL:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4274624/

- **Sample:** 15 pre-literate children, ages 4y2m–5y0m
- **Finding:** Free-form printing (handwriting) uniquely activated brain regions associated with reading — left fusiform gyrus, left inferior frontal gyrus, posterior parietal cortex — during subsequent letter viewing
- **Typing and tracing did NOT produce these activations**
- **Implication:** The variable, non-stereotypical letter forms produced through handwriting appear to support development of abstract letter categories — a building block of reading
- **Limitations:** Tiny sample (n=15), English-only, manuscript print not cursive, measured brain activation not actual reading scores

### 2b. Van der Weel & Van der Meer (2024) — brain connectivity during writing
**Citation:** Van der Weel, F. R., & Van der Meer, A. L. H. (2024). Handwriting but not typewriting leads to widespread brain connectivity: a high-density EEG study with implications for the classroom. *Frontiers in Psychology*, 14, 1219945. https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1219945
**URL:** https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1219945/full

- **Sample:** 36 university students (early 20s) — **NOT children**
- **Finding:** Handwriting produced significantly more elaborate brain connectivity than typing — 32 cluster differences. Theta/alpha coherence in parietal/central regions during handwriting only.
- **Authors' claim:** These connectivity patterns are "crucial for memory formation and for encoding new information"
- **Authors' classroom recommendation:** "Children, from an early age, must be exposed to handwriting activities in school"
- **Critical limitation:** The study was on adults. The classroom recommendation is the authors' inference, not direct evidence from children.

### 2c. Implication for journaling
- A handwritten journal at age 5–11 is a *more* cognitively engaging activity than the same content typed
- The brain-connectivity benefit is more strongly supported by James & Engelhardt's child sample than the adult EEG study
- For pre-readers and emergent readers, even drawing-with-labels journals may carry the handwriting-related cognitive benefits, given the James findings on letter formation

## 3. Dialogue journals — the strongest writing-and-relationship evidence

Dialogue journals (sustained written back-and-forth between teacher and student) are the closest literature to "journaling supports literacy" with direct child evidence. But the rigour is uneven.

### 3a. Peyton (2000) — foundational CAL digest
**Citation:** Peyton, J. K. (2000, December). Dialogue journals: Interactive writing to develop language and literacy. *National Center for ESL Literacy Education* / Center for Applied Linguistics.
**URL:** https://www.cal.org/caela/esl_resources/digests/Dialogue_Journals.html

- **Definition:** "Written conversations in which a learner and teacher (or other writing partner) communicate regularly" on self-selected topics; teacher responds as conversational participant, not evaluator
- **Origin:** First documented systematic use was Leslie Reed's sixth-grade class (1980s California) with native and non-native English speakers
- **Reported outcomes:** Teacher observations describe writing becoming "more fluent, interesting, and correct over time"; vocabulary growth through exposure to proficient adult model
- **Caveat:** These are practitioner reports, not effect-size measurements. The digest does NOT cite controlled trials.

### 3b. Greater Good in Education (UC Berkeley) — practice summary
**Citation:** Greater Good in Education. Dialogue Journals for Elementary Students. (UC Berkeley Greater Good Science Center.)
**URL:** https://ggie.berkeley.edu/practice/dialogue-journals-elementary/

- **Recommended for:** PreK–Lower Elementary and Upper Elementary
- **Outcomes claimed (citing peer-reviewed studies):** writing motivation, self-esteem, problem-solving, self-awareness, social awareness, relationship skills, classroom management, rapport, benefit for second-language learners and introverted students
- **Cautions documented:**
  - Refer to school counselor if a student discloses serious difficulty
  - Don't overwhelm students with corrections or advice
  - **Validate rather than correct** the student's writing — corrective marking changes the activity from journal to assignment

### 3c. Pham & Tran (2021) — quasi-experimental, ages 8–10
**Citation:** Pham, V. P. H., & Tran, T. N. M. M. (2021). Reflecting Journal Writing and Primary Students' Writing Fluency. *Proceedings of the AsiaCALL International Conference*, 621, 1–7.
**URL:** https://asiacall.info/proceedings/index.php/articles/article/view/25

- **Design:** Quasi-experimental, 60 participants (30 treatment, 30 control), ages 8–10, Vietnamese school
- **Intervention:** Reflective journal writing added to vocabulary lessons over 3 months
- **Finding:** Treatment group showed "great effects" on writing fluency and reduction of writing errors
- **Limitations:** No effect sizes published in the conference paper; small sample; EAL context; short duration

### 3d. Honest read on dialogue journals
- The bulk of dialogue-journal evidence is **descriptive and practitioner-driven**, not from rigorous RCTs
- The literature is dominated by ESL/EAL contexts (Peyton's main focus) rather than mainstream native-speaker primary classrooms
- "Few studies since the 1980s and 1990s have dealt with writing in dialogue fashion" (per recent reviews) — the field has not matured into a strong evidence base
- **What we can reasonably claim:** dialogue journals consistently produce *teacher-reported* fluency, vocabulary, and motivation gains; they also build relationship. **What we can't claim:** large measured effect sizes from controlled trials.

## 4. Journaling and executive function / metacognition

### 4a. The "writing as applied metacognition" framing
Reflective journaling is widely cited in education literature as a metacognitive activity — the writer must:
- Hold goals in working memory (what am I writing about, why)
- Inhibit irrelevant content (a self-regulation skill)
- Monitor their own thinking (metacognition proper)
- Shift between strategy and content

These are precisely the executive functions that develop most rapidly from preschool through primary years (working memory, inhibition, cognitive flexibility — see Diamond's framework).

### 4b. EMERGE model and SRL reviews
**Citation context:** Recent syntheses link executive function (EF), metacognition, and self-regulated learning (SRL) into a unified framework — the EMERGE model (2026, Educational Psychology Review).
**URL:** https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-026-10157-0

- Strong correlation between EF and metacognition in school-age children
- Both are foundations of self-regulated learning
- **However:** No meta-analyses I could locate directly test "journaling improves executive function in primary schoolers." The link is theoretically plausible and indirectly supported by SRL/EF literature, but it is not directly evidenced.

### 4c. Honest read
The metacognition argument for journaling in primary school is **theoretically reasonable but empirically thin**. We can say journaling is the kind of activity that *involves* metacognitive skills; we cannot confidently say journaling *develops* metacognition more than other reflective activities (e.g., guided conversation, oral reflection, art-making).

## 5. Cross-cutting observations after Phase 2

1. **Journaling is not a high-evidence literacy intervention.** The K-5/K-3 meta-analyses don't even have enough journaling studies to analyze it as a category. If literacy is the parent's goal, structured writing instruction has 3–5x larger effects.

2. **Handwriting itself appears to matter.** The James & Engelhardt child study (n=15, modest but interesting) shows letter-formation recruits reading-network brain regions in pre-literate children. A handwritten journal carries this neural-developmental benefit; a typed one does not.

3. **Dialogue journals (teacher-student back-and-forth) have the most directly applicable child evidence** for literacy and motivation, but the literature is dominated by practitioner reports and ESL contexts, not RCTs.

4. **"Non-stop writing" intervals are explicitly endorsed by mainstream literacy researchers** (Shanahan). Free-write journaling is functionally identical to this technique. The fluency benefit from generic extra writing is around g = 0.30 in Graham 2012 — small but real.

5. **The metacognition argument is plausible but not directly proven** for primary-age children. Journaling involves metacognitive engagement; whether it builds metacognitive capacity faster than alternatives is not established.

6. **Practical implications for a parent:**
   - If improving writing skill is the goal: structured instruction first, then daily journaling as supplement
   - If using journaling: handwrite, not type, especially at ages 5–9
   - Validate; do not correct (this is the dialogue-journal lesson)
   - Short daily entries beat long weekly ones (Shanahan's distributed-practice point)
   - Don't expect dramatic literacy gains from journaling alone — the effect is real but modest
