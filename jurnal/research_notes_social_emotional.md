# Research Notes — Phase 3: Social-Emotional Evidence

This file covers the social-emotional dimension: anxiety, mood, self-awareness, emotional regulation, and well-being. This is where the case for journaling in primary school is strongest *and* most nuanced — because the **format of journaling matters enormously** for whether it helps or harms.

**Headline takeaway from Phase 3:** The Pennebaker "write deeply about a stressful event" paradigm — the foundation of adult expressive-writing research — does NOT transfer cleanly to primary-school-aged children, and in at least one well-designed study actually INCREASED their anxiety. By contrast, **gratitude-style positive expressive writing produces small-but-real reductions in anxiety and depression** in school-age children (Cohen's d ≈ 0.30–0.45). The mechanism appears to be emotion-regulation maturity: kids whose regulation skills are still developing can struggle to process negative emotions through writing.

This is the most policy-relevant finding in the entire research. **If a parent is considering recommending journaling, the format choice is not cosmetic — it is the difference between a small benefit and a small harm.**

---

## 1. The critical caveat: expressive writing can increase anxiety in children

### 1a. Mesghina & Richland (2020) — the surprising finding
**Citation:** Mesghina, A., & Richland, L. E. (2020). Impacts of expressive writing on children's anxiety and mathematics learning: Developmental and gender variability. *Contemporary Educational Psychology*, 63, 101926.
**URL:** https://eric.ed.gov/?id=ED608916 (open access); https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0361476X20300916

- **Sample:** 250 children, ages 10–12 (M = 11.55), 127 females
- **Design:** Experimental, expressive writing vs control before a high-demand math lesson on ratios
- **Hypothesis (from adult literature):** EW would reduce anxiety and free working memory
- **Actual finding:** **"In contrast to the literature with adults, children assigned to expressive writing had greater anxiety relative to control"**
- **Mediation analysis:** EW reduced learning gains *by increasing* children's anxiety during the lesson
- **Most-harmed subgroup:** Higher-achieving, higher-working-memory girls — they showed *decreases* in immediate learning and retention relative to peers
- **Authors' interpretation:** "Emotion regulation skills may be an under-considered mechanism that underpins benefits of expressive writing in older adolescents and adults"

**Implication:** The Pennebaker paradigm, where you "write deeply for 15-20 minutes about a stressful or emotional experience," should not be uncritically prescribed to primary-school children. Their emotion regulation skills are still developing — putting them face-to-face with stressful material in writing can deepen distress rather than process it.

### 1b. Cross-reference back to Travagin (Phase 1)
The Travagin et al. (2015) meta-analysis already hinted at this: "Children and early adolescents benefit *less* from disclosing negative experiences." Mesghina & Richland sharpened that finding — at ages 10–12, in some contexts, the effect is not just smaller but negative.

### 1c. The other side — Fartoukh & Chanquoy (2020)
**Citation:** Fartoukh, M., & Chanquoy, L. (2020). Expressive writing in school children: Effects on well-being and working memory. *Journal of Writing Research*, 11(3), 505–523.
**URL:** https://www.jowr.org/index.php/jowr/article/view/585

- **Sample:** 5th graders (~ages 10–11)
- **Design:** Four expressive writing sessions over 2 weeks; one group wrote about stressful personal events, other about ordinary school experiences
- **Finding:** *All* children showed decreased depression and anxiety symptoms — but ONLY the EW group showed working memory improvement
- **Contrast with Mesghina:** Sustained 4-session protocol, school-based intervention; not a one-shot pre-test situation. Sustained EW may carry processing benefits a one-shot does not.

**Reconciliation:** EW about negative experiences appears riskier in **acute** settings (just before a stressful task) than in **sustained, supportive** settings. The two studies don't contradict — they suggest *context* moderates the effect.

## 2. The opposite signal: gratitude/positive expressive writing helps

### 2a. Hu et al. (2025) — RCT with positive expressive writing
**Citation:** Hu, C. S., Zhang, H., Hu, L., Nadiiev, A., Yan, J., & Liu, M. (2025). Efficacy of positive expressive writing in reducing depression and social anxiety symptoms among schoolchildren during COVID-19: A randomized controlled trial. *Journal of Health Psychology*, 30(10), 2516–2528.
**URL:** https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/13591053241295446

- **Sample:** 165 Chinese schoolchildren ages 10–15
- **Design:** RCT, three arms — Positive EW (write about people/things grateful for, 10 min weekly × 7 weeks), Memory EW (write about memorable past-week experiences), Control (regular class meetings)
- **Findings:**
  - Positive EW reduced depression vs control, **Cohen's d = −0.45**
  - Positive EW reduced social anxiety vs control, **Cohen's d = −0.45**
  - Positive EW reduced social anxiety more than memory writing, d = −0.32
- **Critical contrast with Mesghina:** Same paradigm category (expressive writing), opposite results — because the content was gratitude-positive, not stress-negative

### 2b. Froh, Sefick & Emmons (2008) — counting blessings
**Citation:** Froh, J. J., Sefick, W. J., & Emmons, R. A. (2008). Counting blessings in early adolescents: An experimental study of gratitude and subjective well-being. *Journal of School Psychology*, 46(2), 213–233.
**URL:** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19083358/

- **Sample:** 221 early adolescents (sixth-grade range, ~11–13 — bottom edge of primary)
- **Design:** 11 classes randomized to gratitude / hassles / control conditions
- **Findings:**
  - Counting blessings → enhanced gratitude, optimism, life satisfaction
  - Decreased negative affect
  - **Robust relationship between gratitude and school satisfaction**, both at post-test and 3-week follow-up
- **Effect sizes not reported in abstract** but cited as foundational by many later reviews

### 2c. UK primary school evidence (from Phase 1, recapped here for completeness)
- **Cripps (BPS)**: Year 6 (~age 11), n = 164. Daily "three things grateful for" diary, two-week intervention. Significant improvements in anxiety, gratitude, school belonging. **No sleep effect.**
- **Diebel (2014)**: Children mean age 9.4. "Trend" toward improved school belonging. Modest signal.

### 2d. PNAS 2025 cross-cultural meta-analysis (recap)
Choi et al. (2025): 24,804 participants, 28 countries. Overall Hedges' g = 0.19 for gratitude interventions. Age did not moderate effects — so the same small positive signal extends across age groups, though primary-school-only subgroups are not isolated.

## 3. SEL framework: what the institutional consensus says

### CASEL framework
**Citation:** Collaborative for Academic, Social, and Emotional Learning. SEL Reflection Prompts. CASEL.
**URL:** https://casel.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/06/SEL-Reflection-Prompts.pdf • https://casel.org/

- CASEL's framework defines five core SEL competencies: self-awareness, self-management, social awareness, relationship skills, responsible decision-making
- Journaling is explicitly recommended as a practice for self-awareness and self-management
- Method: post reflection prompts; allow students to write first; share with partner if comfortable, but **sharing is not required**
- **The CASEL position is that reflection (often written) is a building block of SEL competence — but the evidence cited is more often for SEL *programs* overall (which include many components beyond journaling), not journaling in isolation**

This is an important honest qualifier: CASEL endorses reflective writing as part of SEL programming. It does NOT publish meta-analytic evidence isolating journaling as the causal ingredient.

## 4. Drawing journals for younger children (5–7)

The empirical literature on journaling for ages 5–7 specifically is extremely thin. Most "school-age" studies start at age 9–10. For younger primary:

- The James & Engelhardt (2012) handwriting study (from Phase 2) suggests letter-formation activities — including drawing-with-labels — recruit brain regions involved in reading
- Practitioner consensus in early-childhood education (e.g., NAEYC, Reading Rockets) supports drawing journals or "draw-and-label" notebooks as developmentally appropriate
- **There is no RCT-level evidence specifically on emotional-regulation effects of drawing journals for ages 5–7 that I located**

This is a research gap, not a contraindication. Drawing journals are widely used in primary classrooms; their emotional/literacy effects in young children are *plausible* but *not directly evidenced* in the rigorous literature.

## 5. Patterns across the social-emotional evidence

| Format | Direction of effect | Magnitude | Best-evidenced age |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Gratitude / positive expressive writing** | Positive | Small (g ≈ 0.19–0.45) | Late primary (8–12) |
| **Counting blessings (structured prompt)** | Positive | Small-moderate | Late primary (10–13) |
| **Pennebaker-style stress-disclosure EW, one-shot acute** | **Negative or null** | Up to d ≈ 0.5 harm in subgroups | Negative finding at ages 10–12 |
| **Pennebaker-style EW, sustained 4-session school context** | Positive (anxiety, depression, WM) | Small | Late primary |
| **Dialogue journals (teacher-student back-and-forth)** | Positive | Mostly practitioner-reported | All primary |
| **Drawing journals (early primary)** | Plausible | Not directly evidenced | Early primary (5–7) |

## 6. What this means for emotional regulation specifically

The Mesghina & Richland (2020) result is the most important developmental finding in this literature. It implies:

1. **Emotion regulation skills are still maturing through primary school.** The capacity to "process" a negative experience through writing — which adults can usually do — is not reliably present in 10–12 year olds, let alone 5–9 year olds.

2. **Writing about negative content without scaffolding can intensify, not relieve, distress.** This is the rumination concern from clinical psychology, made concrete in a pediatric sample.

3. **Positive-content journaling (gratitude, accomplishments, things noticed) appears safer and modestly beneficial across the age range.**

4. **If a child is dealing with something genuinely difficult, writing about it in private isn't the right intervention — adult-supported dialogue (oral or written) is the safer scaffold.** Greater Good in Education's dialogue journal cautions echo this: refer to a counselor if a child discloses something serious.

## 7. Open questions to drive Phase 4–5 (practice and risks)

- What does the **rumination** literature add to the Mesghina caveat? (clinical adult evidence)
- What are evidence-based **frequency and duration** parameters?
- What about **parental snooping** — does the privacy of a journal matter for whether benefits accrue?
- For children with diagnosed anxiety, depression, or trauma — when is journaling helpful vs harmful?
- For children with writing difficulties (dysgraphia, EAL, ADHD with handwriting struggles), does mandatory journaling backfire?
