# Research Notes — Phase 4+5: Practice (How To Do It Well) and Risks (When Not To Do It)

This file synthesizes what the evidence says about *operating* a journaling practice with primary-school children: format, frequency, duration, adult involvement, and privacy — alongside the documented risks (rumination, when EW backfires, equity, mandatory journaling).

**Headline takeaway:** The single most-overlooked finding in the journaling literature: **privacy and validation are *not* nice-to-haves; they are mechanisms of effect.** When journals are monitored or corrected, effect sizes shrink. When children feel their writing is private and accepted, effects accrue. The strongest research-backed protocol for a primary-school child is: **gratitude-style content, 5–10 minutes, 2–4 times per week, handwritten, private, never corrected, never read without permission.** The biggest documented risk is using expressive writing about negative content with a child who lacks emotion-regulation skills — it can deepen distress, not relieve it.

---

## 1. The most authoritative dose-response evidence

### Sohal, Singh, Dhillon & Gill (2022) — journaling meta-analysis
**Citation:** Sohal, M., Singh, P., Dhillon, B. S., & Gill, H. S. (2022). Efficacy of journaling in the management of mental illness: a systematic review and meta-analysis. *Family Medicine and Community Health*, 10(1), e001154. doi:10.1136/fmch-2021-001154
**URL:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8935176/

- **Scope:** 20 RCTs, 31 outcomes
- **Conditions:** PTSD, anxiety disorders, depression
- **Overall finding:** **5% reduction in mental health symptom scores** vs control
  - Anxiety: 9% reduction
  - PTSD: 6% reduction
  - Depression: 2% reduction (smallest)
- **Cohen's d:** 16 outcomes at d = 0.2–0.5; 6 outcomes at d ≥ 0.5 — small to moderate
- **Effective parameters:**
  - **15–20 minute sessions** were typical
  - **2–4 sessions** in 85% of studies
  - **>30 days duration** was needed for depression effects
  - **68% of gratitude journal studies showed positive effects** — higher hit rate than expressive writing variants
- **No adverse effects reported** across studies (though see Sbarra 2013 below for a separate dataset that shows them)
- **CRITICAL INSIGHT:** **"When journals are not collected or analysed the efficacy of journaling is greater"** — privacy is a treatment ingredient, not a courtesy
- **Limitations:** B-level evidence (inconsistent), high heterogeneity (I² = 83.8%), no preparatory training sessions in any study, no high-quality longitudinal data

### What this implies for primary-school children
- The Sohal review is heavily adult-leaning, but its parameters are roughly congruent with what worked in the Hu 2025 RCT with schoolchildren (10 min × weekly × 7 weeks = small effect d ≈ 0.45)
- The privacy finding (effect bigger when journals not collected) directly contradicts a teacher- or parent-monitored journaling approach
- For a primary-aged child, the protocol that aligns with this evidence is: **gratitude-focused, short sessions (5–10 min), 2–3 times per week, sustained over weeks, kept private**

## 2. Privacy as a mechanism, not a courtesy

### 2a. Empirical signal: monitoring reduces effect
From Sohal 2022: "When journals are not collected or analysed the efficacy of journaling is greater." This is the single most actionable finding for parents.

### 2b. Developmental psychology: privacy supports autonomy and trust
Drawing on Psychology Today and developmental research syntheses:
**Source:** Lansford, J. E. (2017). No Mom, you can't read your daughter's diary (or texts). *Psychology Today*, Thinking About Kids blog.
**URL:** https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/thinking-about-kids/201702/no-mom-you-cant-read-your-daughters-diary-or-texts

Key findings synthesized from developmental research:
- Parental privacy invasion **predicts reduced parental knowledge** — when parents snoop, children disclose less, not more
- Surveillance can reduce self-esteem; children read "you don't trust me" as "I'm not worthy of independence"
- Chronic privacy violations correlate with heightened anxiety and weakened sense of personal agency

### 2c. Practical recommendation
- **Establish the privacy boundary at the start.** When giving a child a journal, explicitly agree: "I will not read this unless I am genuinely worried about your safety."
- **Honor the boundary even when curious.** The benefit of the practice depends on it.
- **Do not correct spelling, grammar, or content** in a personal journal (the dialogue journal literature is unanimous on this — validate, do not correct, or the journal collapses into another assignment).

## 3. Format effectiveness: gratitude > free expression > Pennebaker-style negative

Compiling across phases:

| Format | Approximate effect size in school-age kids | Risk level |
|---|---|---|
| Gratitude / "three good things" | Cohen's d ≈ 0.30–0.45 (positive) | Very low |
| Drawing or draw-and-label (early primary) | Not directly evidenced; plausibly positive | Very low |
| Free expression (positive or neutral content) | Small positive, around d = 0.20 | Low |
| Dialogue journal (teacher-student, validation-based) | Practitioner-reported positive | Low |
| Pennebaker-style "write about a stressful event," one-shot | Negative in 10–12-year-olds (Mesghina) | **High in acute settings** |
| Pennebaker-style EW, sustained school-context | Positive (Fartoukh & Chanquoy 2020) | Moderate — needs scaffolding |
| Mandatory journal with teacher reading | Reduced effect (Sohal monitoring finding) | Low harm but minimal benefit |

**The actionable rule:** start with gratitude-style prompts. Free expression is fine. Avoid asking a child to write about something stressful or upsetting unless an adult is supporting the processing — and even then, prefer dialogue (oral or written exchange) over solo expressive writing for emotional content.

## 4. Documented risks and contraindications

### 4a. Rumination — the biggest risk
**Citation:** Sbarra, D. A., Boals, A., Mason, A. E., Larson, G. M., & Mehl, M. R. (2013). Expressive writing can impede emotional recovery following marital separation. *Clinical Psychology Science*, 1(2), 120–134.
**URL:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4297672/

- Separated adults assigned to expressive writing had **worse emotional outcomes** than controls
- The most-harmed subgroup: people who already had high trait rumination and were actively meaning-making about their loss
- Authors' conclusion: **"Engaging in expressive writing when a negative experience is unfolding, raw, and still quite upsetting can intensify rather than ameliorate this distress"**

### 4b. The Mesghina parallel in children (from Phase 3)
The Sbarra adult result has a direct pediatric analogue: Mesghina & Richland (2020) found expressive writing INCREASED anxiety in 10–12 year olds during a stressful academic context. The mechanism in children is plausibly the same as in ruminating adults — without scaffolding, the writing keeps the negative state active rather than processing it.

### 4c. Prompt design — what triggers rumination
From the rumination literature:
- **Harmful:** Global self-evaluation prompts ("What's wrong with me?" "Why do I feel this way?")
- **Less harmful, more useful:** Analytical, slightly-distanced prompts ("What happened?" "What might I do next time?")
- **Safest:** Concrete, positive, present-tense prompts ("Three things I noticed today" "One thing that went well")

### 4d. Adverse-event evidence is thin but not zero
- Sohal 2022 reports **no adverse effects** across 20 RCTs — but adverse events are rarely systematically measured in this literature
- Sbarra 2013 demonstrates that in a specific (separated/ruminating) population, harm is detectable
- Mesghina 2020 demonstrates the same in a pediatric academic-stress context

**Honest read:** the literature is not designed to detect harm well. Absence of evidence of harm ≠ evidence of absence of harm.

## 5. Equity, accessibility, and the "what if writing is hard?" question

### 5a. Dysgraphia and ADHD-related writing struggles
- **Dysgraphia affects 5–20% of learners** (Webmd / Cleveland Clinic estimates)
- Frequently co-occurs with ADHD and dyslexia
- For these children, **mandatory handwriting journals can become an anxiety-source rather than a help**
- Accommodations the literature supports:
  - Speech-to-text (Google Voice, Apple Dictation, etc.) as a journaling format
  - Audio journaling (talk to a recorder)
  - Drawing journals with minimal text
  - Shorter sessions
  - No grammar/spelling correction (this is the dialogue-journal rule anyway)

### 5b. English-as-Additional-Language learners
- Most dialogue-journal evidence is actually from ESL contexts (Peyton's foundational work)
- For EAL primary children, dialogue journals appear *more* beneficial than for native speakers — they provide low-pressure language exposure
- Pham & Tran (2021) showed reflective journal writing reduced writing errors and improved fluency in Vietnamese 8–10 year olds

### 5c. Children with diagnosed mental health conditions
- Sbarra 2013 and Mesghina 2020 jointly suggest: **for children with high anxiety, depression, or rumination tendencies, unsupervised expressive writing about negative content is contraindicated**
- For these children: defer to a mental health professional for guidance; journaling-as-therapy is not a do-it-yourself protocol
- The Sohal review found small effects of journaling for clinical conditions (5% symptom reduction) but recommended professional context

## 6. Adult involvement — the Goldilocks problem

The evidence pulls in two directions:

**For:** Dialogue journals (teacher-student written conversation) consistently show motivation and writing-fluency gains — adult involvement is *the* mechanism.
**Against:** Sohal 2022's finding that monitored/collected journals show smaller effects than unmonitored ones — adult involvement *reduces* the mental-health benefit.

**Reconciliation:** The two studies are measuring different outcomes. For literacy and motivation (Peyton), adult engagement helps because the journal becomes a relationship vehicle. For mental health and emotional regulation (Sohal), adult monitoring undermines the safe-space function.

**Practical implication for parents:**
- A child can have *both*:
  - A **private personal journal** parents do not read (for emotional self-regulation, identity work, processing)
  - A **shared dialogue journal** with a parent or teacher that's explicitly two-way (for connection, language modeling, validation)
- These serve different purposes and should not be merged

---

## 7. The "mandatory vs voluntary" distinction

- The Amos 2025 scoping review (from Phase 1) found **implementation contamination** in 6 of 19 studies — children assigned to EW sometimes did not follow instructions (wrote superficial content)
- Mandatory journaling that the child doesn't buy into appears to produce performative writing with minimal benefit
- The Greater Good in Education dialogue-journal guidance recommends **sharing not be required** — children opt into sharing their writing or keep it private

**Implication:** Coercion is anti-therapeutic in this context. If a child resists journaling, mandating it likely produces neither the literacy nor the emotional benefit. Offering, modeling, scaffolding — yes. Forcing — no.

## 9. The bullet-point protocol that falls out of this evidence

For a typical primary-school child (ages 5–11), no diagnosed conditions:

1. **Start small.** 5 minutes, 2–3 times per week. Daily is fine but not required.
2. **Prefer gratitude-style or "things noticed" prompts** over open-ended emotional prompts.
3. **Handwrite, not type** (handwriting recruits reading-relevant brain regions, especially under age 9).
4. **Pre-writers can draw**; emerging writers can draw-and-label.
5. **Privacy is non-negotiable.** Parents do not read without permission. This is a treatment ingredient.
6. **Never correct spelling or grammar.** The journal is not an assignment.
7. **Modeling helps.** A parent who keeps their own visible journal practice normalizes it.
8. **Offer, don't mandate.** Forced journaling produces neither benefit.
9. **For dysgraphic / ADHD / EAL kids:** allow speech-to-text, audio journals, or drawing.
10. **If a child is dealing with something significant** (loss, anxiety, family stress), don't rely on solo journaling — involve dialogue (with a parent, teacher, or counselor).
11. **Sustained matters.** Effects measured in studies are typically at >30 days; expect benefit to be gradual.
12. **Don't expect dramatic outcomes.** The evidence-based effect sizes are small (d ≈ 0.2–0.5). This is a low-cost low-risk practice with modest upside, not a transformative intervention.
